    BEHIND THE FORMULA

    Why Catalan
    numbers appear.

    The coefficient Cm begins as a count. Start with triangles, allow more kinds of pieces, then read the same counting rule as a root equation.

    Back to the worked formula ↑

    Start by dividing a polygon

    Draw noncrossing diagonals in a convex polygon until every piece is a triangle. A triangle has one triangulation; a square has two; a pentagon has five. These are the Catalan numbers: Cn counts the ways to divide an (n + 2)-gon into n triangles. A bare edge supplies the starting case, C₀ = 1.

    Cn=(2n)!(n+1)!n!:1,1,2,5,14,…

    Fix one boundary edge. The triangle touching it leaves two regions, each either bare or triangulated in the same way. Give each triangle a factor t, and collect all the counts in a series C(t). Then:

    C(t)=1+tC(t)2

    The 1 records the bare edge. The square combines the two independent region choices; t records the triangle joining them. This is why a counting problem produces a quadratic equation.

    Now allow different pieces

    Keep the boundary edge fixed, but allow quadrilaterals, pentagons, and larger pieces too. A (k + 1)-sided piece touching that edge leaves k regions to fill. Give that piece a factor tk. The same rule becomes:

    S=1+t2S2+t3S3+⋯+tdSd

    Now mk counts the (k + 1)-sided pieces. Subdivisions with the same piece counts contribute the same product of parameters. Its coefficient Cm counts how many such subdivisions there are: a hyper-Catalan number. Rubine’s polygon construction gives this rule precisely.

    This is the C(1,1) = 5 in a cubic’s order 2. Its numerical contribution is 5t₂t₃, using the selected root’s parameters.

    Where the factorial formula comes from

    Let j = ∑mk count the pieces, K = ∑k mk, and M = ∏mk!, with all indices from 2 to d. Mark each piece with an extra variable z and put U = S(zt₂, …, ztd) − 1. Then:

    U=z∑k=2dtk(1+U)k

    Lagrange inversion extracts coefficients from an equation like this. For j ≥ 1, it says:

    [zj]U=1j[uj−1](∑k=2dtk(1+u)k)j

    The brackets mean “take the coefficient of this power.” Choosing the piece types across the j factors gives j! / M choices. Their powers of (1 + u) add to K; its uj−1 coefficient is the binomial coefficient below.

    Cm=1j×j!M×(Kj−1)=K!(K−j+1)!M

    Since K − j + 1 = 1 + ∑(k − 1)mk, this is the formula in step 3. With no pieces, the separate starting case is C(0,…,0) = 1. With only triangles, m₂ = n and all other counts zero, it reduces to the ordinary Catalan formula above.

    Read the counting rule as a root equation

    Return to the polynomial. The solver shifts and scales the variable, x = a + δy, to put the equation into this form (after removing repeated factors):

    y=1+t2y2+t3y3+⋯+tdyd

    It is the same equation that the counting series S satisfies. Once that series converges, set y = S and undo the change of variable: x = a + δS. The parameters now have numerical values; a negative parameter can make a product negative even though Cm is a positive count.

    The counting rule gives a formal series first: an identity between coefficients. Numerical convergence is a separate question. This lab chooses parameters for each root, certifies local convergence, and checks which root the series reaches. That is why the weights stay the same while the values and error bounds change.

    How the Geode fits in

    The root series is S. The related series called the Geode is G, defined by the formal factorization:

    S−1=(t2+t3+⋯+td)G

    S and G have different coefficient arrays. The worked formula shows the hyper-Catalan coefficients of S; the numerical solver also uses checked Geode refinement. Gessel’s Geode identity explains the connection.

    Read the mathematics ↗
    01

    Simple and repeated roots

    At a real root, the polynomial is zero. Some roots cross the horizontal axis; others turn around there. Repeated roots appear once in the results.

    02

    Counting and isolating roots

    Geode uses exact arithmetic and a Sturm sequence to count and isolate real roots. Checked Geode refinement and bisection then narrow down their locations.

    03

    Precision and absolute error

    Requesting 30 accuracy digits asks for absolute error at most 10−30. MPFR supplies the precision; the graph uses ordinary floating-point numbers and can miss fine detail.

    10−30
    EXACT DECIMAL INPUTS

    Decimal coefficients are exact rational numbers: 0.1 means 1/10. Returned decimal root values approximate roots of exactly that polynomial. Working precision controls the calculations and available accuracy; the graph uses ordinary floating-point arithmetic.
